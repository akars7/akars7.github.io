The question

Everyone says the crowd helps the home team. How much, exactly? When COVID closed the stadiums, the same teams kept playing under the same referees, just without fans. That is as close to an experiment as football gets.

What I did

  • Collected 14,507 matches from the top five leagues, 2015–16 to 2022–23, and joined them with real attendance data.
  • Controlled for team strength with a two-way regression, the same idea basketball analysts use for RAPM.
  • Measured home advantage several ways (goals, points, win rates) so one blowout score couldn't decide the answer.
  • Put a confidence interval on every result, using bootstrap, permutation tests and the delta method.

What I found

60–68%of home advantage disappeared without fans (goals: CI 40–81%)
45% → 40%home win rate. The lost wins went to away teams, not to draws
0.35 → 0.14average home goal margin per match

The home side's yellow-card edge disappeared completely. Away teams stopped getting extra cards, and foul counts did not explain it, which points to referees reacting to the crowd. No single referee stood out. Bookmakers lowered home-win odds, but not enough.

What I learned

Check every sentence against its confidence interval before writing it. The data supports "bookmakers overrated home teams in empty stadiums". It does not support "the market was slow to react".

The question

Some questions need two facts from two different documents. Standard retrieval often finds only one of them. HippoRAG builds a knowledge graph to connect them. Does it really work better?

What I did

  • Implemented the HippoRAG pipeline: fact extraction (OpenIE), named entities, Contriever embeddings and a knowledge graph.
  • Tested it on HotpotQA, a benchmark of multi-hop questions, and compared it with LightRAG.
  • Scored both with precision, recall, F1, MRR, exact match and ROUGE.

Outcome

A solo research paper for a directed study on large language models and knowledge graphs at UVic.

May – Sep 2026 · Victoria, Canada

Health Information Analyst

BC Ministry of Health · co-op

Forecasting how many long-term care beds British Columbia will need over the next ten years.

British Columbia funds long-term care and home care for seniors through its health authorities. The funding is worked out by rate-setting rules, and for years those rules lived in old SAS programs that few people could still read or change.

My main job was to translate that logic into SQL and then prove the new code gave the same answers as the old. I checked the rate calculations one by one, because a small mistake there changes how much money goes to care. The SQL version is easier to maintain and feeds the ministry's reporting and automation work.

I also looked ahead. I forecast how many long-term care beds the province will need over the next ten years and what they will cost, so planners can budget for an ageing population. And I built Power BI dashboards that show five years of temporary rate reduction decisions across the health authorities, then presented the findings to the health authorities and other clients.

Jan – Apr 2026 · Remote

Data Analytics Intern

BaroShift (formerly MyTorso.AI) · co-op

A churn analysis that showed the team which users they could still win back.

BaroShift is a health-tech startup that works with heart and breathing signals. I had two jobs there: understand the users, and understand the signals.

The users. Keeping a user is much cheaper than finding a new one, so the team needed to know who was about to leave. I sorted all 228 users into green, yellow and red by how their usage was changing. The yellow group mattered most: 75 people who were still active but slipping away, and so the ones worth contacting first.

1087545
Green: activeYellow: at risk of droppingRed: dropped

The signals. The product reads the pulse with a PPG light sensor, and that signal gets noisy whenever someone moves. I queried the raw data with SQL in BigQuery and Grafana and built checks that find the noisy parts, so they don't spoil the results.

With clean data, I studied how different breathing ratios change heart rate, heart rate variability (HRV) and resonance frequency, the breathing pace at which a person's heart rhythm responds best. I found patterns across user groups that prepare the ground for a model predicting each user's own resonance frequency.

Apr – Nov 2023 · Istanbul, Türkiye

Business & Data Analytics Intern

Zer Koç

A pricing model for TV advertising, improved from 94.1% to 96.5% R².

Zer is part of the Koç Group, one of Türkiye's largest business groups. The price of a TV advertising slot depends on many factors at once, and at that scale a small pricing error costs real money.

I built a linear regression model in Python (pandas, NumPy) that predicts real TV ad prices. The first version reached an R² of 94.1%. I then used feature importance to see which variables were really driving the price, and used that to decide which features to keep, drop or rebuild. With the improved feature set, R² rose to 96.5%.

Then came the part I learned most from: working with the ad sales team to turn the model into pricing insights they could use in real client conversations. It was my first time explaining a model to people who don't build models. I also worked with the cyber security team on a data-driven analysis of system vulnerabilities.
